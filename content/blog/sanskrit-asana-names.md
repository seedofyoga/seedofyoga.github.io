The teacher says "Parivrtta Ardha Chandrasana" and the room hesitates. A few people look around to see what everyone else is doing. The name lands as a long stretch of unfamiliar syllables, something to be copied rather than understood.

However, these names are not arbitrary labels. They are descriptions, and often instructions. _Parivrtta_ means revolved. _Ardha_ means half. _Chandra_ means moon. _Asana_ means pose. Put them back together and you have Revolved Half Moon Pose — which is precisely what the body is being asked to do. In this article, we will take these names apart word by word, look at the vocabulary they are built from, and work through a series of examples until the longest of them becomes readable.

---

## Asana: the word at the end

**Asana** comes from the Sanskrit root _as_, meaning to sit or to be seated. In Patanjali's [Yoga Sutras](/blog/definition-of-yoga-8-limbs/), asana is the third of the eight limbs, and it is described simply as a stable and comfortable seat — a position the body can hold long enough for the mind to settle. The hundreds of physical postures we practise today came much later, through Hatha yoga, but they kept the word.

Every posture name ends in `-asana`. What makes the names look like one long unbroken word is a rule of Sanskrit called _sandhi_ — sounds that meet at a word boundary merge. _Tada_ (mountain) plus _asana_ becomes _Tadasana_. _Chandra_ (moon) plus _asana_ becomes _Chandrasana_. Once you expect that fusion at the end, the rest of the name separates much more easily.

## The building blocks

Most pose names are assembled from a surprisingly small pool of words. Around forty of them will carry you through an ordinary class.

**Direction and orientation:**

- _adho_ — downward
- _urdhva_ — upward
- _utthita_ — extended
- _supta_ — reclining
- _parivrtta_ — revolved, twisted
- _parsva_ — side, flank
- _ardha_ — half
- _baddha_ — bound
- _salamba_ — supported (and _niralamba_, unsupported)
- _prasarita_ — spread wide
- _upavistha_ — seated
- _ut-_ / _uttana_ — intense, stretched out

**Numbers:**

- _eka_ — one
- _dvi_ — two
- _tri_ — three
- _chatur_ — four
- _ashta_ — eight

**The body:**

- _hasta_ — hand
- _pada_ — foot or leg
- _angustha_ — big toe
- _mukha_ — face
- _janu_ — knee
- _sirsa_ — head
- _bhuja_ — arm
- _karna_ — ear
- _kona_ — angle
- _danda_ — staff
- _paschima_ — west
- _purva_ — east

Those last two need a word of explanation. Yoga was traditionally practised facing the rising sun, so the front of the body is the east and the back of the body is the west. This is why _Paschimottanasana_ — the seated forward bend — is literally an intense stretch of the west, meaning the whole back line of the body.

**Animals and nature:**

- _svana_ — dog
- _bhujanga_ — serpent, cobra
- _kurma_ — tortoise
- _kapota_ — pigeon
- _matsya_ — fish
- _ushtra_ — camel
- _garuda_ — eagle
- _vrksa_ — tree
- _padma_ — lotus
- _chandra_ — moon
- _surya_ — sun
- _tada_ — mountain
- _setu_ — bridge
- _sava_ — corpse

**Sages, gods and heroes:** a smaller group of names points to a person rather than a shape — Virabhadra (a fierce warrior created by Shiva), Marichi, Matsyendra (the lord of the fishes), Hanuman, Vasistha, Bharadvaja. These names carry a story instead of a description, so they cannot be decoded the same way. _Gomukhasana_ sits in between: _go_ is cow and _mukha_ is face, and the pose is named for the shape the crossed legs make.

## Reading a name, word by word

### Tadasana

- _tada_ = mountain
- _asana_ = pose

**Translation:** Mountain Pose.

The simplest possible name — one word and the suffix. Standing still, upright, rooted.

### Salamba Bhujangasana

![sphinx](/images/backbend-asanas/sphinx.png)

- _sa_ = with
- _alamba_ = support
- _bhujanga_ = serpent, cobra
- _asana_ = pose

**Translation:** Supported Cobra Pose, known in class as Sphinx.

_Salamba_ is one of the most useful words to recognise, because it tells you the shape is being held up by something — here the forearms on the floor. The same prefix appears in _Salamba Sarvangasana_, supported shoulderstand.

### Ardha Uttanasana

![Ardha-Uttanasana](/images/forward-fold/Ardha-Uttanasana.png)

- _ardha_ = half
- _ut_ = intense
- _tan_ = to stretch
- _asana_ = pose

**Translation:** Half Intense Stretch Pose, usually called Half Forward Bend.

_Uttanasana_ is the full standing forward bend. Add _ardha_ and you have the halfway version, spine long and hands at the shins.

### Prasarita Padottanasana

![Prasarita-Padottanasana-A](/images/forward-fold/Prasarita-Padottanasana-A.png)

- _prasarita_ = spread wide
- _pada_ = foot
- _ut_ = intense
- _tan_ = to stretch
- _asana_ = pose

**Translation:** Wide-Legged Intense Stretch Pose.

Notice how _pada_ and _uttanasana_ have merged into _padottanasana_ — the same sandhi rule at work in the middle of the name rather than at the end.

### Supta Kurmasana

![Supta-Kurmasana](/images/forward-fold/Supta-Kurmasana.png)

- _supta_ = reclining, sleeping
- _kurma_ = tortoise
- _asana_ = pose

**Translation:** Sleeping Tortoise Pose.

_Supta_ appears constantly, and it is a reliable signal that you will be on your back or your front rather than standing: _Supta Baddha Konasana_, _Supta Padangusthasana_, _Supta Virasana_.

### Parivrtta Ardha Chandrasana

- _parivrtta_ = revolved
- _ardha_ = half
- _chandra_ = moon
- _asana_ = pose

**Translation:** Revolved Half Moon Pose.

Three modifiers, and each one changes the shape. _Chandrasana_ gives the crescent. _Ardha_ makes it the half moon balanced on one leg. _Parivrtta_ turns the whole thing, bringing the opposite hand to the floor and the chest toward the ceiling.

### Triang Mukha Eka Pada Paschimottanasana

![Triang-Mukha-Eka-Pada-Paschimottanasana](/images/forward-fold/Triang-Mukha-Eka-Pada-Paschimottanasana.png)

- _tri_ = three
- _anga_ = limb
- _mukha_ = face
- _eka_ = one
- _pada_ = leg
- _paschima_ = west, the back of the body
- _uttana_ = intense stretch
- _asana_ = pose

**Translation:** Three-Limbs-Facing One-Legged Intense West Stretch Pose.

Five words and a suffix, and nothing in it is new. Three limbs — foot, knee and thigh — face backward while the other leg extends forward into a one-legged forward bend.

## The order of the words

The words are not stacked at random. They follow a fairly consistent order, working from the outermost qualifier inward to the shape itself:

1. **Variation or rotation** — _ardha_, _parivrtta_, _utthita_, _supta_, _prasarita_
2. **Support or bind** — _salamba_, _baddha_
3. **Number and body part** — _eka pada_, _dvi pada_, _hasta_, _janu_, _sirsa_
4. **The subject** — _chandra_, _kapota_, _svana_, _kona_
5. **_asana_**

This is why the names can be read from left to right, like a sentence that narrows as it goes. _Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana_: extended, hand, big toe, pose. _Eka Pada Rajakapotasana_: one, leg, king, pigeon, pose. Once the pattern is familiar, an unfamiliar name is rarely a wall.

## A note on pronunciation

A rough guide, offered as a practitioner rather than a Sanskrit scholar. Vowel length matters: the short _a_ sounds like the _a_ in "about", while the long _ā_ sounds like the _a_ in "father" — so _asana_ is closer to AH-sa-na than to a-SAH-na. The _th_ in _Uttanasana_ or _hatha_ is an aspirated t, never the English "th" of "the". And _ch_ in _chandra_ is the _ch_ of "church".

If you want to hear it properly, listen to a teacher who has studied the language and copy them. Reading it off a page will only get you so far.

## Conclusion

The vocabulary is small and it repeats. Learn _ardha_, _supta_, _parivrtta_, _eka pada_ and a dozen animals, and most of what a teacher says stops being a foreign sound to imitate and becomes something closer to an instruction — revolved, half, moon. That shift is worth the small effort of learning the words, because it changes what you are able to hear in class.
