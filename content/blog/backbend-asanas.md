Where forward bends turn inward, backbends open outward. They stretch the front of the body, strengthen the spine, and tend to be energizing rather than calming. However, they require preparation — the spine needs to be warmed before going deep into these poses.

There is also something else they require, something less often named: a willingness to be exposed. The front of the body is where the organs are, where the throat is, where the heart is. Opening it is not something the nervous system finds instinctively safe. This is why backbends can feel charged in ways that standing or seated postures do not.

---

Backbends work primarily through the extensor chain — the muscles along the back of the body that lengthen and lift the spine into extension. Erector spinae, rhomboids, gluteals: all of them engage and strengthen as the front body opens. The chest, the belly, the hip flexors, the anterior shoulders — these are the structures being asked to yield.

The nervous system's resistance is not irrational. The front of the body is where we are most vulnerable, and the instinct to protect it is old. Backbends gently — and over time, more deeply — ask that instinct to soften. The work is physical, but the quality of the work is something closer to trust. This is not metaphor. It is what practitioners consistently notice: a kind of held breath, a moment of bracing, before the chest begins to open. Learning to breathe through that moment is a significant part of what backbends teach.

### Sphinx Pose — *Salamba Bhujangasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the belly with the forearms on the floor, elbows directly under the shoulders and forearms parallel. Press the forearms down to lift the chest. The pelvis and legs stay on the floor throughout. The lower back should feel long rather than compressed — the lift comes from the upper back and chest, not from crunching into the lumbar. The gaze is forward and slightly down, the back of the neck long.

**Benefits:** *Salamba Bhujangasana* is the gentlest supported backbend in the repertoire. The Sanskrit *salamba* means "supported" — the arms carry the weight, which means the back muscles are not yet asked to do the work alone. This makes it the ideal starting point before moving toward Cobra. It opens the chest and allows the cervical spine to gently extend without demanding much from the lumbar region. Practiced at the beginning of a backbend sequence, it warms the tissue, steadies the breath, and gives the nervous system time to adjust to the sensation of the front body opening.

### Cobra Pose — *Bhujangasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the belly with the palms on the floor beside the lower ribs. Without pressing into the hands, lift the chest by engaging the back muscles. The pubic bone stays on the floor. The arms may straighten partially depending on the depth of the backbend available — the elbows stay close to the body and the degree of lift follows the back's capacity, not the arms' strength. The hands are a support, not a lever.

**Benefits:** *Bhujangasana* strengthens the entire posterior chain — back extensors, glutes, the deep muscles of the spine — while opening the chest and anterior shoulders. The name refers to a cobra raising its head: what matters is the quality of the lift from within, not the push from the arms. It is a mild but effective backbend, accessible relatively early in a practice, and one of the clearest demonstrations of a principle that runs through all backbends — that the pose opens by engaging what is behind, not by collapsing what is in front.

### Locust Pose — *Salabhasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the belly with the arms alongside the body, palms facing down or upward. On an inhale, lift the legs, chest, and arms simultaneously off the floor. The gaze travels forward and slightly down, with the back of the neck long. The entire posterior body engages at once — there is no part of the back body that is passive in this pose.

**Benefits:** *Salabhasana* is one of the most direct poses for strengthening the back extensors, and particularly useful for practitioners who spend long hours seated. Unlike *Bhujangasana*, Locust asks the body to lift without hand support. What lifts is what must be strong. The pose does not allow for any borrowing of effort from elsewhere: the back must do what the back must do. There is a quality of self-sufficiency here that is instructive beyond the physical. Held in stillness, it also builds tolerance for the unfamiliar sensation of the back body fully contracted — something that becomes necessary in deeper backbends.

### Upward-Facing Dog — *Urdhva Mukha Svanasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the belly with the hands beside the lower ribs. Press the hands and the tops of the feet firmly into the floor and lift the entire body so that only the palms and feet remain in contact with the ground — the thighs lift entirely off the floor. The arms straighten. The chest opens forward and the gaze travels toward the horizon. The key distinction from Cobra is that lift: the thighs are off the ground, which means the whole body hangs suspended between the hands and feet.

**Benefits:** *Urdhva Mukha Svanasana* is simultaneously a strong backbend and a significant chest opener. It strengthens the wrists, arms, and the entire back, and it appears regularly in flow sequences as the counterpose to Downward Dog — the two shapes forming a rhythmic alternation between extension and flexion. It requires wrist strength and shoulder mobility, and it is worth building both before arriving here. When practiced with full attention — chest genuinely lifting rather than simply the head being craned back — it has an opening quality that is noticeably energizing.

### Bow Pose — *Dhanurasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the belly and bend the knees, then reach both hands back to hold the outer ankles. On an inhale, kick the feet into the hands to lift the chest and thighs off the floor simultaneously. The body rocks forward onto the belly like a tightened bow. Keep the knees approximately hip-width — they will want to splay outward, and keeping them parallel requires deliberate attention. The breath continues through the belly even as the belly presses into the floor.

**Benefits:** *Dhanu* means bow — the hands are the string, the body is the arc, and the image is precise. *Dhanurasana* opens the chest, the shoulders, and the hip flexors together, while strengthening the back. The rocking motion on the breath can feel massaging to the abdominal organs in a way that is unlike any other pose. It asks for a kind of surrender to the shape: once the hands hold the feet and the kick begins, the body takes a form it cannot fully control. The result is both exhilarating and humbling.

### Bridge Pose — *Setu Bandha Sarvangasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the back with the knees bent, feet hip-width apart and parallel, arms alongside the body. Press both feet evenly into the floor and lift the hips. Optionally, clasp the hands under the back and roll the shoulders underneath to broaden the chest from below. *Setu* means bridge and *bandha* means lock or bind — the body spans the space between feet and shoulders. The feet press down, the hips rise, and the spine extends.

**Benefits:** *Setu Bandha Sarvangasana* is a moderate backbend — accessible to most practitioners and useful across a wide range of intentions. It opens the chest, strengthens the legs, glutes, and lower back, and can be used therapeutically for lower back tension when practiced with attention to the pelvis and the breath. It is also a genuinely restorative pose when held passively over a block. Less intense than the prone backbends, it asks for steadiness rather than depth. In the arc of a backbend sequence, it often serves as a transitional pose — the body still warm from the floor work, but beginning to orient upward.

---

### Camel Pose — *Ustrasana*

**Main cues:** Kneel with the hips directly over the knees, thighs vertical. Place the hands on the lower back with fingers pointing down and the elbows drawing toward each other behind you. Lift the chest and begin to bend back. Practitioners with greater mobility can reach the hands to the heels; tucking the toes under raises the heels and makes this more accessible. The head follows the spine naturally — it does not lead the movement.

**Benefits:** A deeper kneeling backbend, *Ustrasana* opens the entire front of the body from the ankles to the throat. Emotionally, this is one of the most significant of the backbend poses. Exposing the chest and the throat is not instinctive — and some practitioners notice strong responses here, responses that are not always proportional to the physical sensation. This is worth knowing before the pose, not as a warning but as a preparation. The nervous system is doing something it does not always do willingly, and that deserves acknowledgment. Always include a counterpose — *Balasana* (Child's Pose) — immediately afterward, and let the body return to itself before moving on.

---

### Wheel Pose — *Urdhva Dhanurasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the back with the knees bent and feet hip-width, then place the palms on the floor beside the ears with the fingers pointing toward the shoulders. Press both hands and feet simultaneously into the floor and lift into a full arch. The elbows tend to splay outward — keeping them shoulder-width apart requires active attention. Straighten the arms and legs as fully as the body allows. The neck is long; the head hangs freely.

**Benefits:** *Urdhva Dhanurasana* is the full expression of spinal extension. It requires and develops shoulder flexibility, hip flexor length, wrist strength, and total back strength at the same time. The demand is systemic — no single area carries the load, and no single area can compensate for weakness elsewhere. This is also what makes it the most clarifying pose in the sequence: it shows clearly what is ready and what is not. It should only be attempted when the preparatory postures — *Bhujangasana*, *Setu Bandha Sarvangasana*, *Ustrasana* — are genuinely established. Rushed, it can strain. Prepared for, it offers a sense of opening that is difficult to describe and unmistakable when it arrives.

---

Backbends are not a destination so much as a direction — a sustained movement toward openness that the body learns incrementally, over many practices. The sequence matters. The preparation matters. Going slowly is not timidity; it is how trust is built — trust in the spine, trust in the breath, and eventually, a quieter trust in the instinct to keep the front body protected. That instinct does not disappear. It simply becomes less urgent, over time, when the body has learned that opening is safe.
