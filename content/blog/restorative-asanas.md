Restorative postures look, from the outside, like doing nothing. The body is still, the effort is invisible, and the shapes themselves are often simple enough that a child could arrive in them without instruction. This appearance is misleading. What happens in a restorative pose is not absence — it is active reception. The body is being asked to release something it has been holding, sometimes for years, and that release does not happen automatically just because someone lies down.

The paradox at the centre of restorative yoga is this: in order to rest deeply, the practitioner must be willing to stay. Not to sleep, not to drift away, but to remain present inside the stillness long enough for the nervous system to shift registers — from sympathetic activity to parasympathetic ease.

---

What makes a posture restorative is not the absence of effort. It is the quality of support. Restorative postures use props — blankets, bolsters, blocks, straps — to hold the body in shapes that require nothing of the muscles. The musculature, having nothing to brace against, eventually releases. The joints open not through force but through time. And the organ being worked is not the hip or the spine or the hamstring. It is the nervous system. Restorative yoga is the practice of teaching the body that it is safe to let go. That teaching takes patience. The body has learned its habits of tension over a long time, and it does not abandon them in a single session. But it does, slowly, begin to trust.

### Child's Pose — *Balasana*

**Main cues:** Kneel on the floor and fold the torso forward over the thighs, bringing the forehead to rest on the mat or on a folded blanket. The arms can extend forward or lie alongside the body with the palms facing up. A block under the forehead softens the neck and releases the need to hold the head. In the wide-knee variation — knees separated, big toes touching — the belly can release between the thighs rather than pressing against them. Remain for one to three minutes, breathing into the back body.

**Benefits:** *Balasana* is the resting posture of the practice — the shape the body returns to when it needs to pause within an active sequence. It releases the lower back and sacrum gently and passively, without any muscular engagement required. The contact of the forehead with the floor has a notable calming effect on the nervous system, one that practitioners often notice within a few breaths. *Bala* means child — the fetal quality of the pose is deliberate. There is something in this shape that the body seems to remember.

### Happy Baby Pose — *Ananda Balasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the back and draw the knees toward the armpits. Hold the outer edges of the feet with the hands and let the knees sink toward the floor on either side of the torso. The lower back remains in gentle contact with the mat rather than lifting. A slow, easy rocking side to side is welcome if it feels natural. There is no destination in this posture — the instruction is simply to settle into the shape and let the weight of the legs do the work.

**Benefits:** *Ananda Balasana* is a gentle hip opener and sacrum release. What distinguishes it from standing hip openers is the supine position: lying on the back removes the need for the body to stabilise against gravity, and the muscles of the inner groin can relax in a way that they simply cannot while standing or seated. The cradling of the legs creates a quality of ease that is rare in adult practice. *Ananda* means bliss — the pose has a playfulness that the rest of the sequence rarely invites.

### Reclined Butterfly — *Supta Baddha Konasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the back and bring the soles of the feet together, allowing the knees to fall open to the sides. Support the outer thighs with blocks or folded blankets if the inner groins are tight — the objective is for the hips to release without the legs working to hold themselves in position. The arms rest alongside the body or extend overhead. An eye pillow deepens the effect. Remain for three to five minutes, or longer.

**Benefits:** *Supta Baddha Konasana* offers a deep inner groin and hip release that requires no muscular effort at all. In the seated version of *Baddha Konasana*, the groin muscles are still working subtly to hold the shape; the reclined version removes that last layer of holding entirely. It is consistently among the most effective restorative postures for calming the nervous system, and it appears regularly in yoga nidra sequences precisely for this reason. The combination of the open hip shape and the supine position signals ease to the body in a way that few other postures can replicate.

### Reclined Hero Pose — *Supta Virasana*

**Main cues:** Begin in *Virasana* — seated between the heels with the knees together on the floor. From there, lower the torso backward onto the elbows and then, if the body allows it, onto the floor. Bolsters or folded blankets beneath the torso make this accessible before the full recline is possible. The knees remain together and in contact with the floor throughout. Any sensation of compression in the knees is a signal to come out immediately — this posture requires genuine flexibility in the quadriceps and ankles, and it will not yield its benefits to force.

**Benefits:** *Supta Virasana* provides an intense and fully supported quadriceps stretch and hip flexor opening in a position that removes all muscular effort from the equation. Where the upright version requires the practitioner to hold themselves in the fold, the reclined version allows the body to sink into the stretch over time, guided by gravity rather than intention. It is particularly valuable for counteracting the patterns of compression that develop through running, cycling, or long periods of sitting — places where the hip flexors shorten slowly, invisibly, until the restriction becomes unmistakable.

### Supine Spinal Twist — *Supta Matsyendrasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on the back and draw one knee to the chest. Guide that knee across the body to the floor on the opposite side, while extending the same-side arm along the floor at shoulder height. Let the gaze fall away from the crossed knee. Place a block or blanket beneath the crossed knee if it does not reach the floor — the objective is for the body to rest completely, not to work toward the floor. Let gravity do the work. Hold for two to three minutes on each side.

**Benefits:** *Supta Matsyendrasana* passively decompresses the lumbar spine and gently releases the outer hip and sacroiliac joint — all without any active holding or effort on the part of the practitioner. This is the quality that makes it genuinely restorative rather than simply restful: the spine is being opened, not just rested. The combination of the twist with the turned gaze tends to quiet the nervous system rapidly. It sits naturally between active practice and *Savasana*, and many practitioners find the transition out of it and into stillness easier than any other route.

### Legs Up the Wall — *Viparita Karani*

**Main cues:** Sit sideways close to a wall and swing the legs up as the torso lowers to the floor. The backs of the legs rest against the wall, and the hips can rest either touching or slightly away from the baseboard depending on hamstring length — neither is correct, and both are fine. The arms spread wide at the sides, palms facing up. A folded blanket under the lower back can deepen the support. An eye pillow transforms the posture into something that can hold a practitioner for ten minutes without restlessness.

**Benefits:** *Viparita* means inverted, *karani* means action — this is "the inverted action pose." It reverses the gravitational pooling of fluid in the lower legs and feet that accumulates over the course of a day, without requiring the muscular engagement of a full inversion. The effect on fatigue is immediate and significant. The nervous system calms quickly in this position, making it one of the most practical restorative postures available — effective, accessible at almost any level, and requiring only a wall.

### Corpse Pose — *Savasana*

**Main cues:** Lie flat on the back with the legs slightly wider than hip-width apart and the feet falling open naturally. The arms rest a few inches from the sides of the body, palms facing up. A blanket over the body in a cool room prevents the drop in body temperature from ending the relaxation prematurely. An eye pillow over the eyes deepens the withdrawal from external stimulation. The body is completely still. The mind remains awake — present but not engaged, aware but not pursuing.

**Benefits:** *Savasana* is the final posture of almost every yoga practice, and without it, much of what the body and nervous system have been preparing for has no time to complete. The practice does not end when the effort stops. It ends — or rather, it begins to integrate — in the stillness. *Sava* means corpse: the pose is a practice in letting go completely, in releasing the identity of the practitioner along with the tension in the muscles. Many teachers consider it the most difficult posture in the sequence, not because the body has to do anything, but because the mind has to stop. Five minutes is the bare minimum. Ten to fifteen is a genuine minimum. The body, given enough time, will remember what it is to be still.

---

The integration that *Savasana* offers is not exclusive to the final posture. It is what the entire restorative sequence is building toward — a nervous system that has been given enough time and support to shift out of effort and into ease. This shift does not happen on command. It happens slowly, with repetition, with the body gradually learning that it is permitted to rest. Each restorative posture, held long enough and supported fully, contributes to that learning. The practice is not dramatic. The postures are not impressive. What they offer, in the end, is the experience that the body has been working toward without knowing it — a rest that is complete.
