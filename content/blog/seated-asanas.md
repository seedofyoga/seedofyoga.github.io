Seated postures ask something different of the body than standing or inverted poses do. There is no balance to manage, no ground to push away from. The floor receives you entirely — and what remains is the work of finding length, openness, and steadiness within that stillness.

For many practitioners, seated postures are where the practice becomes genuinely interior. The nervous system quiets. The breath slows. And the body begins to reveal exactly where it holds tension, and where it does not yet know how to release.

---

Seated poses share a common foundation: the pelvis. Whether the legs are extended, crossed, folded, or stacked, what matters most is how the pelvis orients itself over the sit bones. When it tilts forward — into a gentle anterior tilt — the lumbar spine can lengthen naturally. When it tucks under, the lower back rounds and the work of sitting upright becomes effortful rather than sustainable. This is why props are not a sign of limitation in seated practice. Sitting on a folded blanket is often the single adjustment that makes a posture genuinely possible.

The floor, in all of these poses, is the teacher. It offers feedback the hands cannot: where the weight is uneven, where one hip is tighter, where the breath catches. A skilled seated practice begins with learning to listen to what the floor says.

---

### Staff Pose — *Dandasana*

**Main cues:** Sit with both legs extended straight in front of you, feet flexed so the soles press toward an imaginary wall. Press the thighs gently but firmly into the floor — this is not passive; the legs are active throughout. Place the hands beside the hips, fingers pointing forward, and use that contact to lift the spine. If the lower back rounds immediately, sit on a folded blanket until the pelvis can tilt forward.

**Benefits:** *Dandasana* is the seated equivalent of *Tadasana* (Mountain Pose) — the foundational form from which all other seated postures are measured. It reveals quickly how much muscular effort is required to simply sit upright: the back extensors, the deep abdominals, the hip flexors all engage together. The pose does not look dramatic, and that is precisely its teaching. Sustained uprightness — whether in posture or in attention — asks more of us than we often expect.

### Easy Pose — *Sukhasana*

**Main cues:** Sit cross-legged on the floor or, preferably, on a folded blanket or firm cushion to tilt the pelvis forward. The shins cross in front without the feet needing to tuck under the knees. The key is a neutral pelvis — not tucked under, not aggressively arched — which allows the spine to rise from a stable base. Let the hands rest on the knees or in the lap, palms open or closed.

**Benefits:** *Sukhasana* — from the Sanskrit *sukha*, often translated as "ease" or "space" — is the traditional posture for *pranayama* and meditation. It is comfortable, the tradition tells us, only when the hips are sufficiently open. For most practitioners, especially those who spend long hours in chairs, some elevation under the hips transforms this pose from a strain on the lower back into a genuine rest. Over time, the pose builds the hip mobility and spinal endurance that longer periods of sitting require.

### Hero Pose — *Virasana*

**Main cues:** Kneel with the knees together and the feet wider than hip-width, toes pointing back. Lower the hips to sit between — not on top of — the heels. If there is any sharp sensation in the knees, place a block or a folded blanket under the hips to reduce the angle. The tops of the feet press evenly into the floor. From a neutral pelvis, the spine lifts.

**Benefits:** *Virasana* is one of the few seated poses that works the front of the body rather than the back — stretching the quadriceps, the tibialis, and the ankles instead of the hips and hamstrings. It is also distinctly calming for the nervous system, and has been used for *pranayama* and *mantra* practice across traditions. The Sanskrit *vira* means hero — not in the martial sense, but in the sense of one who is present and willing. The pose asks for attention, not force.

---

### Lotus Pose — *Padmasana*

**Main cues:** Each foot rests on the opposite thigh, sole facing upward, with the ankle supported by the thigh rather than hanging in space. Both knees rest toward the floor. This posture requires the hips to be genuinely open before it is attempted — if there is any pressure in the knees, the hips are not yet ready. Begin with *Ardha Padmasana* (Half Lotus), one foot at a time, until the range of motion is available.

**Benefits:** *Padmasana* is the meditation posture of Buddhist and Hindu traditions alike. The stable, symmetrical base it creates — the body locked into a triangular form — supports extended periods of sitting without the attention being drawn back to the body. When it is genuinely available, it is among the most grounding of all postures. But it is a destination, not a starting point, and many dedicated practitioners never need to arrive there. The depth of a meditation practice is not measured by the position of the legs.

### Bound Angle Pose — *Baddha Konasana*

**Main cues:** Sit with the soles of the feet together and the heels drawn in toward the pelvis. Allow the knees to open toward the floor — without pushing them. Sit on a blanket if the lower back rounds. Hold the feet with both hands, letting the spine lengthen on each inhale. The inner groins release gradually; they do not need to be pressed.

**Benefits:** *Baddha Konasana* — *baddha* meaning bound, *kona* meaning angle — describes both the geometry and the intention of the pose. It is one of the most consistent and gradual openers for the inner hips and groins, areas that respond poorly to force and well to patient, sustained presence. The pose calms the nervous system in a way that more effortful postures do not, and is considered in the tradition to be beneficial for the kidneys and the reproductive organs. What it requires most, however, is simply the willingness to stay.

---

### Cow Face Pose — *Gomukhasana*

**Main cues:** Stack the knees vertically — one directly above the other — with the feet wide on either side of the hips. Sit on a blanket if the hips are uneven (they almost always are at first). The lower leg delivers a deep external rotation stretch to its hip; let the weight of the leg do the work rather than pulling. The arm variation — one arm reaching overhead and behind, the other reaching up the back — can be practiced independently from the leg position.

**Benefits:** *Gomukhasana* — go meaning cow, mukha meaning face — targets the deep external rotators of the hip: the piriformis and its neighbors, muscles that are rarely addressed in other postures. These are precisely the muscles that shorten and tighten from long hours of sitting. The arm variation simultaneously opens the chest, the anterior shoulder, and the axilla in a way few poses replicate. Together, the full pose is one of the most complete antidotes to the postural effects of modern sedentary life.

---

### Fire Log Pose — *Agnistambhasana*

**Main cues:** Stack the shins directly — the ankle of the top leg rests on the knee of the bottom leg, and the knee of the top leg rests above the ankle of the bottom leg. Flex the top foot firmly to protect the knee joint. Sit upright, and if the top knee is far from the floor, place a block underneath it rather than collapsing the spine to compensate. The posture is intense; the breath is the tool for working with that intensity.

**Benefits:** *Agni* means fire and *stambha* means pillar or log — the image is precise. The pose targets the piriformis and the deep gluteal muscles with a directness that even *Gomukhasana* does not always match. Significant sensation in the outer hip is entirely normal; sharp pain in the knee is not, and that distinction matters. Like fire, the pose transforms what it touches — and what it tends to touch, in most practitioners, is a store of held tension that has been there a long time.

---

The seated postures do not ask you to go anywhere. They ask you to arrive more fully where you already are — on the floor, in the body, in this breath. That quality of arrival is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. But over time, in the quiet geometry of these forms, it becomes something the body begins to know how to do.
