Twisting asanas work along a different axis than most of the practice. Where forward folds lengthen, and backbends expand, twists rotate — drawing the spine around its vertical center in a way that wrings the torso, compresses the abdominal organs, and then, on the release, floods them with fresh circulation. That quality of compression followed by release is what makes twists distinct. They are not simply stretches. They are a kind of internal movement.

---

What unites all twisting postures is the mechanics of spinal rotation — and a principle that applies across all of them: the spine must lengthen before it can rotate. Trying to twist a compressed spine is like trying to wring a bunched towel. The first task is always to create length. Inhale to grow tall; exhale to turn. This is not just a cue. It is the architecture of the pose. In the yogic tradition, twists are associated with the digestive organs — the compression and release is said to stimulate them in much the same way that kneading wakes up dough. Whether or not one holds that view literally, the wringing quality of twists produces a genuine effect: after them, the body feels both lighter and more integrated.

### Bharadvaja's Twist — *Bharadvajasana*

**Main cues:** Sit with both legs swept to one side, as if sitting sideways off a chair. Place one hand on the opposite knee and the other behind you on the floor. Inhale to lengthen the spine from the tailbone to the crown — do not skip this step. On the exhale, rotate the torso. The twist originates in the thoracic spine, the middle and upper back, not the lumbar. The lower back stays relatively stable while the ribs and chest turn.

**Benefits:** *Bharadvajasana* is one of the more accessible seated twists, but accessible does not mean shallow. The asymmetrical hip position — both legs on the same side — creates an effective opening in the sacroiliac area and the outer hip of the folded leg. Because the lumbar spine is not asked to rotate deeply, the pose is commonly used in therapeutic sequences for lower back pain and stiffness. Named after the sage Bharadvaja, one of the seven great *rishis* of the Vedic tradition, it carries in its name something of what it offers: steadiness and absorption.

### Half Lord of the Fishes — *Ardha Matsyendrasana*

**Main cues:** Sit with one leg extended and the other bent, foot placed on the floor outside the extended leg's thigh. Ground both sit bones. Inhale to sit as tall as possible — this moment of length determines how deep the rotation can go. Exhale and rotate toward the bent knee, bringing the opposite elbow to the outside of that knee as a lever. The extended leg stays active, foot flexed. For the full variation, the extended leg bends into half-lotus; the seated twist remains, but the base deepens.

**Benefits:** *Ardha Matsyendrasana* is the classical seated spinal twist — the pose most teachers introduce when they teach twists at all. It works the spine's full rotational range in a way that few other postures match, and it counteracts the compression that accumulates from long hours of sitting. The name is one of the tradition's more evocative: Matsyendranath, the "lord of the fishes," is said to have overheard Shiva teaching yoga to Parvati while in the form of a fish hidden beneath the sea. The half variation carries that story lightly, but it carries it.

### Sage Twist — *Marichyasana III*

**Main cues:** Sit with one leg extended and the other bent, foot planted close to the hip. Inhale to lengthen the spine — the extended leg and its grounded heel are the stable base that makes this possible. Exhale and rotate toward the bent knee, wrapping the opposite arm around the outside of that knee. If the range is available, bind the hands behind the back. The torso lifts on each inhale and deepens on each exhale, rather than holding a fixed position throughout.

**Benefits:** *Marichyasana III* is one of four poses named after the sage Marichi, son of Brahma — the third variation being the twisting one. It asks for more hip and hamstring openness than *Bharadvajasana* in order to establish its base before any rotation begins, and the twist itself is deeper. The compression of the torso against the thigh creates the wringing quality that the tradition associates with digestive stimulation. What the pose requires in order to do its work is the willingness to be patient at the foundation — length first, rotation second, binding last.

### Revolved Chair Pose — *Parivrtta Utkatasana*

**Main cues:** Begin in Chair Pose (*Utkatasana*): feet together or hip-width, knees bent, thighs angling toward the floor, arms raised. Keep the thighs where they are — this is essential — and bring the palms together at the chest. On an exhale, rotate the torso and bring one elbow to the outside of the opposite thigh, using the contact as a lever for deeper rotation. Both knees stay level throughout. The thighs do not rise.

**Benefits:** *Parivrtta Utkatasana* combines the considerable heat of Chair Pose with a thoracic twist, which makes it significantly more demanding than any of the seated variations. The legs are not passive — they are working hard — and that engagement is what gives the pose its distinctive quality. The effort in the lower body and the rotation in the upper body occur simultaneously, and navigating both requires a degree of coordination that is, in itself, the practice. The pose is often described in the tradition as detoxifying in a more active sense than passive twists: the whole body is engaged in the wringing.

### Revolved Triangle — *Parivrtta Trikonasana*

**Main cues:** From a standing split stance — front foot forward, back foot turned in — extend the torso forward over the front leg and rotate, bringing the opposite hand to the floor or a block inside or outside the front foot. The back arm extends upward. The natural tendency of the hips is to unwind in the direction of the twist; resisting that tendency — keeping the hips as square as possible — is the central work of the pose. Length in the spine precedes rotation here too.

**Benefits:** *Parivrtta Trikonasana* combines spinal rotation with the lateral elongation of Triangle Pose, which makes it one of the more technically demanding postures in a foundational standing practice. It requires genuine thoracic rotation — not just a twist at the shoulders — and develops that capacity over time. The counter-rotation through the hips, the resistance against their natural unwinding, is what creates the therapeutic tension in the spine. The pose teaches the body to hold two opposing instructions at once: open this way, resist that way, stay long throughout.

---

### Revolved Side Angle — *Parivrtta Parsvakonasana*

**Main cues:** From a lunge with the front knee bent deeply over the ankle, rotate the torso and bring the opposite elbow to the outside of the front knee. The back knee can stay on the ground for a more supported variation, or lift for greater challenge. The top arm extends overhead alongside the ear, and from the back foot to the fingertips, the body traces a single long diagonal. The front knee provides a lever for the elbow that allows the rotation to go deeper than it can in Revolved Triangle.

**Benefits:** *Parivrtta Parsvakonasana* is a deeper and more demanding version of its standing counterpart — it asks for simultaneous engagement of the legs, stability in the hips, and full rotation in the thoracic spine. Balance is an active concern; the body is working in multiple directions at once. The elbow-to-knee contact creates a pressure that, when used wisely rather than forced, offers a rotation that is difficult to achieve through any other means. In the tradition, it is among the poses most associated with the purifying effect of deep, sustained twisting.

### Supine Spinal Twist — *Supta Matsyendrasana*

**Main cues:** Lie on your back. Draw one knee to the chest, then guide it across the body and down toward the floor on the opposite side. Extend the arm of the same side out along the floor and turn the gaze in the opposite direction. Let gravity do the work. There is no need to press the knee toward the floor with the hand, nor to force the shoulder down. The pose finds its depth on its own, over time, through the weight of the body and the steadiness of the breath.

**Benefits:** *Supta Matsyendrasana* decompresses the lumbar spine passively and completely — it is the supine counterpart to all the seated and standing twists, and it offers a depth of rotation that many practitioners find difficult to access in any upright position. Because the floor supports the weight of the body, the muscular effort of holding the pose is absent. What remains is the pure quality of the twist: the gentle wringing of the lower back, the soft compression of the abdomen, and the release when the legs return to center. It is used as both a closing pose and a transitional one, and it is, in both contexts, an act of letting go.

---

Twists sequence well when they follow the work they are meant to release. After standing poses, after backbends, after deep hip openers — the twist arrives as a kind of completion. It reorganizes what has been opened. It returns the spine to its axis. And the supine twist at the end, with gravity doing the work and the breath settling, is often where the body finally accepts what the practice has been asking of it throughout. The compression was never the destination. The release always was.
